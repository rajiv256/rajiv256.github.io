# The Sandpaper Gate – The scandalous scandal in Cricket

The recently released autobiography by Usmaan Khawaja (an Aussie cricketer), titled “SABR”, triggered a firestorm in Australian cricket, bringing into light an 8 year old “Sandpaper Gate” scandal from under the memory wraps. For the uninitiated, in the second Test match between Australia and South Africa, Cameron Bancroft (“Bangers”), an Aussie cricketer was caught on live camera using a sandpaper to change the way a cricket ball acts, more commonly referred to as “ball tampering”. *Off-topic: This is why cricket is so interesting. It’s not just about the batsman and the bowler. It’s about the pitch, it’s about how old the ball is, it’s about how the weather is. There are a lot of variables that affect the game play\! This way, the team has to change tactics mid-way. No other sport can beat cricket in terms of strategy and tactics. Love Cricket.* 

# **Background**

Here’s a picture of a cricket ball for visualization. Because we are going to visualize a lot.  
*![][image1]*

In Cricket, there are broadly two ways in which a bowler bowls: (i) pace or fast bowling—this is ball is pitched at faster speeds ranging from 120 kph to 150 kph; and (ii) spin bowling — ball is pitched at slower speeds ranging from 80 kph \- 100 kph but involves spinning the wrist to extract a trajectory change once the ball bounces off the pitch. Reminder that it is the norm in Cricket to bounce the ball unlike Baseball where the pitching is totally through the air. 

Each bowling technique involves its own tactics that utilize the condition of the pitch, the condition of the ball, and weather conditions such as breeze, and if it is overcast. *Cricket is a thing of art.* We will talk primarily about Pace bowling in this blog. 

In pace bowling, *swing* is a commonly deployed tactic to bamboozle the batsmen and extract a wicket in the process. Swing, as the name suggests, implies that the ball deviates from its original expected trajectory after it is bowled. Before we go into swing bowling, let’s talk about Bernoulli’s principle, the one that dictates how airplane wings are designed. [(see link)](https://pilotinstitute.com/bernoullis-principle/) In one sentence, consider an air as a “flow”, a higher flow velocity creates a low pressure pocket and a lower flow velocity creates a high pressure zone, which generates the lift from high pressure zone to the low pressure zone. Diverging paths, where one path is longer than the other creates such a pressure difference. 

![][image2]

Now, how can this make the ball swing? The answer is complicated and is the topic of this blog. Swing is not uni-dimensional. It is affected by many many co-acting and sometimes opposing factors. 

## ***Case 1: Early Swing—When the ball is new***

When the ball is new, both sides possess equal “shine”. Due to this, swing is created by tilting the seam (the bulge in the middle) towards or away from the bowler. The ball swings towards the tilt. While the mechanics of this tactic is complicated, the back rotations of the ball creates uneven pockets due to the tilt. Therefore, the wrist rotation while bowling to rotate the ball around the “inclined” seam axis, results in swing, away or towards the batsman. The former is called *outswing* and the latter is called *inswing.* 

## ***Case 2: Conventional Swing—When the ball starts getting older***

You can commonly see the fielding team rubbing the ball against their thighs to rough one side of the ball. Here, the idea is to create roughness on the ball’s surface one one half compared to the other. Here, the roughness creates turbulence on its end, leading to a low pressure pocket compared to the shiny side. This either combines or counteracts with the seam-based swing depending on the positions of the rough side and the seam. Typically, turbulence-based swing dominates and the ball swings towards the rough side. There are four different combinations and each produces swing depending on their combined pressure difference generated\! Friggin mind-boggling and exciting\!

## ***Case 3: Reverse Swing — When the ball is really old i.e., if the seam is mostly gone***

This typically happens in Test Cricket, where an innings can go one for days together—don’t worry they take breaks to have lunch, to have tea, before the end of the day at 5:30pm. As the ball keeps getting older, the seam is completely lost. Then, depending on the roughness, we could expect the ball to swing either to the rough side and surprisingly sometimes, towards the shiny end, depending on how the bowler bowls the ball. This is the more treacherous kind of swing, which makes a delivery absolutely unplayable. The curvature of the ball could become like a flatter ‘S’ reversing the trajectory, or might swing more to the same side, bamboozling the batsman. # **Now, to the Controversy**

So, snapping back to 2018, in a Test series between Australia and South Africa, an Australian cricketer, Cameron Bancroft, was caught on camera using a sandpaper, to rough up the ball in hopes of extracting reverse swing early rather than in the later parts of the game. This is illegal behavior, as you cannot “tamper” with the ball. Several ball tampering incidents have happened, and the players usually got away with it with match bans or warnings. However, as it always is, the cover-up is worse than the crime. They played it off as a “yellow tape” used to stick dust particles on the ball. But Bancroft along with then captain Steven Smith (Smudge) lied to the reporters about this being the first time and that they will not repeat this. 

However, there was footage of the sandpaper being used, which created a firestorm in the cricketing community. Cricket Australia (CA) stepped in, investigated the case, and ruled that the blast radius was a group of three people: Bangers, Smudge, and then Vice Captain David Warner. Smudge and Warner were given one year bans, while Bangers was given a 9 month ban. The bowler group of four people: Pat Cummins (Pat), Josh Hazelwood (HazelGod), Mitchell Starc (Starky), and Nathan Lyon (Gaza), released a statement denying that they knew about the sandpaper.  And the world moved on to more exciting things in cricket. 

Cut to the present, Usmaan Khawaja (Uzzie), then member of the Aussie cricket team, released his memoir titled ‘Sabr’, in which he recollected the incident saying “EVERYBODY KNEW” and that the blame was placed on only three people. Warner promptly backed this revelation. Bangers said, “I won’t say more on this topic at this time”. Usmaan further mentioned that his point is that people were unfairly blamed and all the team should take responsibility for their disservice and then move on. 

Now pressure mounted on Pat, then lead bowler and now captain to answer as to the veracity of Uzzie’s claims. He stayed silent for a week and then had a marathon press conference where he squarely denied having any knowledge, accused Uzzie of making false accusations and having an ulterior motive of increasing his book sales. This explosive press conference, drew an equally explosive newspaper column by Bangers, where he elaborated on what happened, and that it was not the first time tampering happened, and that Smudge didn't want any part of it. I repeat, Smudge, didn’t want any part of it. And yet, he came infront of the media, took all the blame for it as a captain, resigned in disgrace, and was ridiculed for it every day for several years. WHAT A GOAT. I repeat, WHAT A LEADER\! Taking blame for something he never did. 

The saga is still ongoing. On Nov 25 (today), Uzzie gave interviews responding to Pat’s interview. He said he took some time off to avoid pouring kerosene on the issue and to give it some time before he talked publicly. Today, I also saw another interview by Faf DuPlessis (Faf), a South African cricketer on how he was caught messing up with the ball and was given a stern warning, saying that he felt bad for Bangers and Smudge.
